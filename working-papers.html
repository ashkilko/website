Studies in progress, with awards and selected conference presentations noted under each study. Free versions are on SSRN (the Social Science Research Network), a public repository where researchers post their studies.

  1. On the effects of continuous trading with I. Indriawan and R. Pascual · Revise and resubmit, Management Science
    Presented atWestern Finance AssociationStern/Salomon Microstructure Conference
    Free version (SSRN)
    Abstract

    The continuous limit order book, in which messages are processed one by one in the order of receipt, is a prominent design feature of modern securities markets. Theoretical models show that this design imposes a cost on liquidity providers and suggest that this cost may be reduced by switching to batch auctions. We examine a recent opposite move, whereby a stock exchange switches from batch auctions to continuous trading. The move leads to a significant increase in adverse selection, which is partly offset by a reduction in inventory costs. The net liquidity effect of the switch is negative.

  2. Who benefits from securities exchange innovation? with K. Sokolov and E. Yelagin · Revise and resubmit, Review of Finance
    Free version (SSRN)
    Abstract

    Securities markets continuously innovate to keep pace with technology. It is often debated if such innovation is beneficial, and which market participants capture the benefits. We contribute to this debate by examining liquidity effects of a wide range of proprietary products and services introduced by exchanges in the United States between 2003 and 2017. Exchange innovation is generally associated with liquidity improvements for those investors who trade in small quantities. The effect is opposite for institutional investors; their trading costs increase, and their market participation declines.

  3. Shades of segmentation and retail execution quality with A. H. Dyhrberg, B. Hagströmer and I. Werner
    Full text coming soon
    Abstract

    The way retail marketable orders are handled differs across market designs. We compare retail execution quality in Canada, where retail orders are largely pooled on exchanges, and the U.S., where they are mostly segmented off-exchange. Pooling involves a trade-off. The advantage is access to a broader set of liquidity providers, and we show that this lowers retail trading costs. The drawback is that pooled retail orders are priced as if they carry the adverse selection of the average order, which we find raises their costs. On balance, the drawback appears to dominate: for the same stocks traded on both sides of the Canada-U.S. border, retail effective spreads are more than one third higher in Canada.

  4. Nocturnal trading with G. Eaton and I. Werner
    Hillsdale Investment Management - CFA Society Toronto Research Award
    Presented atAmerican Finance AssociationEuropean Finance Association
    Free version (SSRN)
    Abstract

    Although still relatively new, nocturnal trading in U.S. equities, defined as trading between 8:00 p.m. and 4:00 a.m., has grown rapidly. It is largely retail-driven, concentrated in a small set of securities, and marked by substantial order imbalances. Using unique transaction-level data, we show that nocturnal execution costs exceed regular-hours benchmarks but are broadly consistent with elevated adverse selection faced by liquidity suppliers. Nocturnal returns generally do not reverse during the subsequent regular-hours session, except in a subset of high-sentiment stocks. Overall, the nocturnal session appears to be an important source of price discovery and may create profit opportunities for retail liquidity demanders despite higher transaction costs.

  5. Multi-asset market making with I. Roşu, E. Sojli and W. W. Tham
    Presented atNBER Market Structure
    Free version (SSRN)
    Abstract

    We develop a dynamic theory of portfolio market making, solved in closed form for any number of assets and dealers. In equilibrium, market makers cross-hedge: their quotes in one asset respond to inventory in another. Cross-hedging increases with asset correlation and has an inverted U-shape in risk aversion. Estimates that ignore cross-hedging can substantially understate risk aversion. Inventory management can lower high-frequency return correlations, consistent with the Epps effect. Unlike investors in standard portfolio theory, market makers do not directly choose their inventory, so those with low risk aversion can prefer highly correlated assets, which hedge each other.

  6. Seasonalities in liquidity and price discovery with M. Kamstra and L. Kramer
    Presented atEuropean Finance Association
    Full text coming soon
    Abstract

    Liquidity costs and the flow of information into prices follow persistent yet distinct seasonal patterns. Liquidity costs peak in early winter and reach their lowest levels in spring, while price discovery is most intensive in early fall and least intensive in spring. These patterns are substantial and closely linked to seasonal shifts in market participants’ impatience and risk aversion, which are influenced by changes in daylight exposure. Seasonality in liquidity is a global phenomenon, most pronounced at higher latitudes where annual daylight variation is greatest, absent near the equator, and predictably offset by six months in the Southern Hemisphere.

  7. Cross-subsidizing liquidity with S. Foley, A. Liu, K. Malinova and A. Park
    Full text coming soon
    Abstract

    Liquidity in modern markets is supplied by voluntary providers who concentrate on large, actively traded stocks, leaving thousands of small stocks illiquid. We study a Toronto Stock Exchange program that bundles market making assignments: a market maker must serve four inactive securities for every active one. Around the staggered rollout, small-stock trading costs fall 12%, entirely through lower liquidity provider rents, with no deterioration in large stocks. Revenue estimates show that direct incentives leave small-stock market making at best break-even; the profits on the bundled large stocks make the assignments worthwhile. The effects persist through 2026 and survive the COVID-19 crash and two program redesigns.
